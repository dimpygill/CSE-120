Creative Problem Solving

In-Class Team Assignment: Improve the Student Experience
45 minutesTeam size: 4No programming required40 points
Purpose. Creative problem solving is the process of understanding a problem, exploring different possibilities, evaluating ideas, and developing a practical solution. In professional environments, teams rarely receive problems with one obvious answer. This activity gives you practice in creativity, critical thinking, communication, collaboration, decision-making, and working within real constraints. Your goal is not to guess the instructor’s preferred answer. It is to explain how your team arrived at a thoughtful, feasible solution.
Detailed sample solution: View the complete worked example: Reducing Long Cafeteria Lines → The example follows the same $500 budget, two-week pilot, evaluation, and reflection requirements. Use it as a reference, not as an answer to copy.

Your Challenge

Your team has been asked to improve one aspect of the student experience at a college. Choose one of the following problems, or another realistic college problem your team can clearly explain:

  • Finding available, quiet study spaces
  • Long cafeteria or coffee-shop lines
  • Limited or confusing parking options
  • Difficulty keeping track of assignment deadlines
  • Confusing campus signs or difficulty locating services
  • Another everyday student challenge

Scenario constraints: You have a hypothetical budget of $500, and your solution should be possible to pilot within two weeks. You may use existing college facilities or free tools, but explain any permissions or cooperation required. You do not need to spend the full budget. You are proposing a solution, not actually implementing it today.

How Your Team Will Work

Work as a team of four. Each member must contribute ideas and participate in discussion. Assign the following starting responsibilities; everyone still helps with every step:

ResponsibilityWhat this person does
FacilitatorKeeps discussion focused, invites input from every member, and manages time.
Idea GeneratorEncourages unusual ideas and helps the team consider multiple approaches.
Critical EvaluatorAsks about feasibility, costs, risks, and unintended consequences without dismissing ideas prematurely.
Recorder / SpokespersonRecords the team’s work and summarizes the final proposal to the class if invited.

Team agreement: Listen without interrupting; build on others’ ideas; do not criticize ideas during initial brainstorming; give everyone a chance to speak; disagree respectfully and explain your reasoning.

Assignment Tasks

Part 1 — Define the Problem (5 minutes)

  1. Identify the problem your team selected.
  2. Explain who is affected and how they are affected.
  3. Explain one possible cause of the problem. Clearly distinguish assumptions from facts you actually know.
  4. Write a “How might we…” question that invites more than one possible solution.

Example of a question format (not a required topic): “How might we help students ______ while keeping costs low?” Avoid questions that already prescribe the answer, such as “How might we build an app?”

Team response: Problem, affected users, cause/assumption, and “How might we…” question.

Part 2 — Brainstorm Creatively (10 minutes)

  1. Spend the first 2 minutes thinking individually. Each team member proposes at least one idea.
  2. Share and record at least five distinct solutions. Include at least one low-tech or no-tech idea.
  3. Do not evaluate or reject ideas during this phase. Consider combining ideas, using existing resources differently, or changing the process rather than purchasing something.

Part 4 — Develop a Practical Plan (10 minutes)

Turn your chosen idea into a realistic proposal. Address all six questions:

  1. Solution: What exactly will change? How will students use or benefit from it?
  2. Implementation: List 3–5 steps to start a pilot within two weeks.
  3. Budget: Estimate costs by item. Keep the total at or below $500. State assumptions (for example, existing equipment is available at no cost).
  4. People and permission: Who would need to approve or support the pilot?
  5. Risk: What could go wrong, and what is your backup plan?
  6. Testing: How would you test the solution on a small scale? Give two measurable indicators of success (for example, average waiting time or percentage of positive survey responses).

Part 5 — Prepare a Team Summary (5 minutes)

Prepare a 60-second summary that your team could present if called upon. Include: (1) the problem, (2) your chosen solution, (3) why you selected it over alternatives, and (4) how you would test whether it works. You are preparing the summary during this period; presentations may be shared or sampled at the instructor’s discretion.

Team response: Four short summary points.

Part 6 — Individual Reflection (5 minutes)

Each student writes their own answers. Respond briefly but specifically:

  1. What idea did you contribute, and how did the team respond?
  2. Did someone suggest a different perspective that changed your thinking? Explain.
  3. How did your team handle disagreement or choose between alternatives?
  4. What did you learn about creative problem solving and communication?
  5. What would you do differently in your next group project?

There is no single correct reflection. You may describe difficulties honestly. Use examples from your actual discussion.

Complete Worked Example — Reducing Long Cafeteria Lines

How to use this example. This fictional example shows the depth and type of reasoning expected in all six assignment parts. It is not the only correct approach, and the costs, baseline measurements, and projections are illustrative assumptions. Your team should create its own ideas and explain its own decisions.

Scenario and Team Responsibilities

Selected problem: Long cafeteria lines during the lunch rush. Constraints: No more than $500 in new spending; a small pilot must be possible within two weeks; existing staff, space, and free college tools may be used only with permission.

RoleIllustrative contribution
FacilitatorInvites each member to contribute and ensures discussion stays on schedule.
Idea GeneratorSuggests alternative pickup routes and signage.
Critical EvaluatorQuestions staffing, accessibility, food safety, and the budget.
Recorder / SpokespersonDocuments the evaluation and prepares the final summary.

Part 1 — Define the Problem (5 minutes)

Problem statement: At peak lunchtime, students purchasing ready-to-go food may wait in the same queue as students ordering meals that require preparation. Mixing different types of transactions may slow the line and create confusion.

Who is affected? Students with short breaks between classes, cafeteria workers, and students who need accessible, predictable service.

Possible causes: A single ordering/payment flow, unclear menus, or bottlenecks at checkout. These are hypotheses, not established causes. Staff observation would be needed to confirm them.

How might we question: “How might we shorten the lunchtime wait for students buying quick items without increasing staffing or spending more than $500?”

Part 2 — Brainstorm at Least Five Solutions (10 minutes)

Each team member suggests at least one idea before the group evaluates them. No ideas are rejected during brainstorming.

IdeaDescriptionCreative angle
1Create a clearly marked express pickup area for prepackaged items, using an existing register when staff approve.Separate short transactions from complex orders.
2Post a large, easy-to-read menu before the ordering point.Help students decide before reaching the counter.
3Offer a limited “quick lunch” combination during the busiest 30 minutes.Reduce decision time and preparation variation.
4Place floor arrows and queue signs to guide students into clearer lines.Improve the existing process without new technology.
5Use an existing college form for advance lunch selections, with staff approval.Move some decisions earlier in the day.
6Display suggested quieter lunch times on campus announcements.Spread demand across available time windows.

Most unexpected idea: Publishing less-busy times does not make the checkout faster, but could reduce congestion by changing when some students arrive.

Why these ratings? The advance form scores low on feasibility because cafeteria staff would need to manage advance selections and pickup logistics. Menu signage scores high on affordability and feasibility, but may have only a modest effect if payment is the real bottleneck. Express pickup could have a larger effect for quick purchases, provided the cafeteria has capacity to operate it.

Selected solution: A one-week pilot of an express pickup area for prepackaged food, supported by simple signs. Trade-off: It may help only students buying eligible items, and it could make the regular queue worse if existing staff are reassigned without planning. The pilot must not assume extra staff are available.

Part 4 — Develop a Practical Plan (10 minutes)

What changes?

With cafeteria management approval, students buying designated prepackaged items use a marked express pickup point. Students ordering prepared meals continue through the usual line. Existing staff decide whether and when the express point can operate safely; if a separate checkout is not possible, the pilot switches to menu and queue signage only.

Implementation steps — pilot within two weeks

  1. Days 1–2: Request approval and observe the lunch rush. Record wait times for a small sample of customers, with no personal identifying data.
  2. Days 3–4: Ask cafeteria staff which products could qualify and whether the existing layout and checkout flow support a separate pickup point.
  3. Days 5–6: Prepare signs, floor markers, and a simple observation sheet. Check accessibility and emergency exit clearance.
  4. Days 7–8: Run a limited lunch-period pilot with staff supervision; record wait times and problems.
  5. Days 9–10: Review the results, ask for feedback, and recommend continuing, revising, or stopping the idea.

Test the idea and measure success

Baseline: Before the pilot, measure the time from joining the line to completing payment for a small sample of customers. For illustration only, assume a baseline median wait of 12 minutes. This is not a real observation.

MeasureProposed success criterionHow to collect it
Wait for express-eligible purchasesMedian wait at least 25% lower than the pre-pilot baseline (12 → 9 minutes or less, if baseline is 12).Time a comparable sample during similar lunch periods.

What if results are disappointing? Ask whether signs were confusing, too few items qualified, or checkout capacity was the bottleneck. Revise one factor and run a smaller follow-up test. Do not claim the solution worked until results support it.

Part 5 — Sample 60-Second Team Summary (5 minutes)

“We focused on long cafeteria lines during lunch. We brainstormed six ideas, including clearer menus, advance ordering, and an express pickup area. After comparing affordability, feasibility, student benefit, and creativity, we selected an express pickup pilot for prepackaged items. Our estimated allocation is $350, below the $500 limit. We would request staff approval, test the idea within two weeks, and compare waiting times before and during the pilot. We would also watch the regular line to make sure we do not create a new problem. If a separate pickup point is not feasible, our backup is clearer signage and queue guidance.”

Part 6 — Sample Individual Reflection (5 minutes)

This is one fictional student's perspective. Actual reflections must be individual and truthful; team members should not copy this paragraph.

“I started as the Critical Evaluator. I initially preferred an advance-order form because it seemed innovative, but another member pointed out that staff might not have time to manage it. That changed my thinking: a creative idea still needs to be workable. We disagreed about whether the express line would slow down other customers. We resolved the disagreement by adding a test of the regular line, not just the express line. I contributed the backup plan. I learned that listening to concerns can improve an idea rather than weaken it. Next time, I would ask more questions about real-world constraints earlier.”
